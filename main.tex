\section{Introduction}
Recommender systems today enjoy a vital role in the modern world which is primarily driven by commerce. They provide a bridge between the user's inability to explore the incomprehensible amount of options available and the business requirement to create value by aligning each user to the best suited item. The items in this scenario can take various forms, from products on an e-commerce website to digital content on a social media platform.

\subsection{Impact of recommendation systems}
Current systems typically rely on multiple recommendation engines on the same platform, each catering to either a different business objective or a different user demographic. In a study conducted in 2014, Lee et al. \cite{Lee2014ImpactOR} explored their effects on revenue, volume, and diversity using data from a field experiment of movie sales. Different systems enable recommendation for different use cases, such as viewing items frequently bought together or viewing items bought by similar users, each of which affects sales and item discovery differently. They can also complement or even replace conventional item search on a platform. YouTube, for example, drives $70\%$ of its engagement using recommendations \cite{TR_YT_Blog}.

Another impact of recommendation systems is that they not only drive users to their preferred items but also shape their preferences. As shown in \cite{Lee2014ImpactOR}, without manual intervention, recommendation systems can inadvertently concentrate on the most popular items. Stakeholders and policy makers can intervene and tune these systems to control the distribution of users, items, and their interactions on the platform. In a recent work \cite{DCC_RecSysImpact}, the authors explore how platform owners can incentivize high-quality content on a digital content platform by biasing the recommendation system appropriately.

\subsection{Conversational recommendation systems}\label{crs}
With advancements in natural language processing (NLP) techniques (and most recently LLMs), conversational search has emerged as an intuitive way for a user to interact with the system. This enables users to query an item by using natural language to define the desired attributes or use case, or any item similar to the one they are seeking. In contrast with traditional keyword based search, conversational search allows for multi-turn queries and advanced context processing making it easier to narrow down to the most relevant item. In \cite{ConvSearchSurvey}, the 4 pillars of a good conversational search system were identified - query reformulation, search clarification, conversational retrieval, and response generation. As we shall see, some of these pillars are also the backbone of an efficient conversational recommendation system.

Conversational recommendation systems (CRS) aims to merge recommendation systems with conversational search delivering personalized recommendations to users using synthetic dialogues in natural language. They improve upon conventional systems by allowing multi-turn dialogues which enables the user to elicit preferences in much more detail, and provide corrections and suggestions to the system. The system can then utilize this information to improve its output by utilizing the updated context. CRS extends beyond academic research, with practical applications spanning e-commerce platforms, entertainment services, virtual assistants, and specialized domains such as healthcare and education. The ability to conduct natural language conversations for recommendation addresses fundamental limitations of traditional systems, including the cold-start problem, modeling preferences dynamically, and the need for explainable recommendations.

\section{Foundational concepts} \label{foundation}
A CRS primarily consists of two major components - a recommender module and a conversational dialogue engine. The recommender module can be considered analogous to first three pillars of a conversational search system as stated in Section \ref{crs}. The objective of this module is to identify user preferences from the multi-turn conversation and utilize the same to search, retrieve and rank the most relevant items for recommendation. 
The dialogue system is aimed at identifying user preferences, guiding the conversation with the task of recommendation and providing a natural experience for the user to interact with the system. Using the dialogues in the conversation, important context about the user and the item being sought can be established. However, the amount of context available in a conversation might be overstated as such conversations are typically much shorter in practice. From a user experience standpoint, it is also ideal to provide the user with the most relevant recommendation with as few queries as possible. Consequently, many researches have tried to supplement the recommendation with external data sources. The information contained in such external sources varies but can include preferences explicitly stated by the users, item attributes, knowledge graphs linking entities often found in conversations to a set of items, and item reviews among others \cite{c2crs}. The availability of such external sources may too be limited. Additionally, it may not be possible to seamlessly integrate this data considering the varying format and semantics they may occur in. In case of unavailability of external sources, a CRS may require large amount of conversational data to train its parameters which makes training a CRS a major data problem as illustrated in the next section.

\section{Datasets}
Unlike traditional recommendation systems where a vast amount of user-item interaction data can be extracted from the platform, most CRS require curated conversational data to train them which is rare. There have been several attempts at synthetic data generation coupled with actual user-item interactions but they always possess a risk of being a poor representation of the real world scenario.

\subsection{Popular datasets} \label{populardata}
\subsubsection{ReDial}
ReDial \cite{redial} is a dataset with over 10,000 multi-turn conversations centered around movie recommendations. The entire dataset only involves dialogues in natural language with at least 10 dialogues and 4 movies mentioned in every conversation.

\subsubsection{TG-ReDial}
TG-ReDial \cite{tgredial} is a dataset with over 10,000 conversations generated in a semi-automated manner. Unlike ReDial where the goal of the conversation is pre-defined, TG-ReDial aims to simulate more realistic conversations by guiding the dialogues to a series of topics beginning from greetings with the end goal being a movie recommendation based on the user profile. This smoothens the flow of the dialogues as they transition from chit-chat to movie recommendations.

\subsubsection{INSPIRED}
Inspired \cite{inspired} is a much smaller conversational dataset consisting of only 1000 multi-turn natural conversations centered around movie recommendations. All dialogues in this dataset have been annotated to highlight any sociable strategy during conversations that can be leveraged for identifying user preferences and generating better contextual responses. Recommender systems can generate responses that empathize with the user's preferences or illustrate expertise about the topic by stating factual information. The objective is to engage the user with a better experience and make them more acceptable of the final recommendation.

\subsection{Dataset Approaches}
As stated in Section \ref{foundation}, conversational data is exceedingly rare in the real world. To counteract this, in popular datasets such those mentioned in Section \ref{populardata}, dialogues are often generated by annotators using user profiles from the historical data. A common approach is to use two entities - one acting as a seeker and one as a recommender \cite{redial, tgredial, inspired}. Both then engage in a conversation with different objectives. The goal of the seeker is to get recommendations based on their profile while the goal of the recommender is to understand the seeker's preference and generate relevant recommendations. This process is laborious and time consuming. Hence, to generate a sizable corpus, this generation is often crowd sourced to entities such as Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) \cite{redial, inspired}. In \cite{tgredial}, the role of the recommender is played by a chatbot with the objective of keeping its responses aligned to the topic of the conversation. Often constraints are established for the generated conversations to make them more useful for training and evaluating CRS. ReDial, for instance, limit the conversation to a minimum of 10 messages and to only about movie recommendations. The relevance of such constraints with respect to real world usage needs to be studied.

Most of the publicly available datasets in the field of CRS are limited to movie recommendations. Agentic CRS systems can directly utilize data from MovieLens without the need for conversational data \cite{MACRS}. Since they primarily rely on external LLMs (such as ChatGPT) for message generation, such systems are tasked with providing the correct context that generates the relevant prediction without having the need to process the entire conversation. For the others, data needs to be generated using the seeker-recommender model as described above. Since this work is crowd-sourced and movies are much easier to converse and reason about, they form the bulk of high quality data available in this field. User profiles are created based on historical data about the movies watched by the user and the humans are tasked with simulating a conversation seeking recommendations based on these profiles.

There have been attempts to generate data for CRS in a multi-modality setting too. Qu et al. \cite{VCRS} established a framework to generate audio conversation data for voice-based CRS by converting interaction data from clothing and movie datasets to text based conversations using ChatGPT templates. Those templates are then converted to speech using a text-to-speech engine factoring in user profiles. This framework, despite being less natural, is much more dynamic than the prevalent crowd-sourcing approach allowing for much faster iteration in this field.

\section{Architectures}
With the rise of transformers, most dialogue systems rely on available LLMs to facilitate conversations with the user \cite{llmimpact}. Consequently, CRS architecture can be broadly defined in 2 major categories - agentic and non-agentic.

\section{Metrics and Evaluation}
Evaluating CRS differs significantly from evaluating traditional recommender systems. While in the latter, most metrics are focused solely on the final recommendation, CRS also needs to be evaluated on its conversational aspect. The quality and the efficiency of the dialogue system in CRS plays a major role in user experience which can both improve or worsen engagement on the platform. Evaluation of the recommendation tasks is identical to that of conventional recommender systems with the objective of rating the quality and relevance of the recommendation made. Assessing the conversational aspect is very subjective and is often done via automatic evaluation strategies or by using human evaluators. In the following sections, we explore both modules separately. All models except MACRS \cite{MACRS} have utilized one or more datasets mentioned in Section \ref{populardata}. MACRS, being an agentic system, relies on MovieLens data.

\subsection{Recommender evaluation}
$Recall@K$ is the most common method to evaluate the performance of the recommender module in CRS. $Recall@K$ tells us whether the most relevant item appears in the top-K predictions of the recommender. $Recall@K$ is sometimes measured with $K = 1$ which indicates the perfect next item recommendation. Other values of $K$ range from $5$ to $50$. \textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)}, used in \cite{TSCR}, is another important metric that also takes in account the rank at which the target item occurs. This prioritizes accuracy of recommendations ranked higher.

On the other hand, \cite{MACRS} focuses heavily on the recommendation being accepted. It defines a metric \textbf{Success Rate} which denotes the percentage of conversations where recommendation by the system was accepted by user within a maximum threshold of turns. To quantify scenarios where the recommendation was not accepted, the authors look at \textbf{Hit Rate @K} which takes in account if the target item was present in the list of final recommendations. 

\subsection{Conversation evaluation}
There is no consensus on the evaluation of the conversational aspect of CRS. Each model approaches this from a different viewpoint leading to a variety of metrics. For instance, \cite{c2crs} measures the diversity of generated responses using the count of distinct n-grams in the responses.
Since a conversational module is not part of the model in \cite{MACRS}, the authors only look at \textbf{Average Turns} used by the dialogue system. A lower number of turns suggest better preference elicitation and is related to a user-centric design in CRS \cite{usercentriceval} but this metric is missing from most research papers.

\subsection{Human evaluation}
Human evaluation of CRS is often considered the most effective approach but is often coupled with high cost and effort. This means that most works utilize a very small set of evaluators limiting the usability of the results. $C^2-CRS$ model, for instance, uses just 3 annotators who rate the responses of their conversational module on Fluency and Informativeness.

Authors of CRB-CRS \cite{CRBCRS} have instead opted for an entirely crowd-sourced evaluation of their model where human participants were recruited via AMT. The participants were asked to label the responses of CRS trained on ReDial using a 5-point scale meant to denote meaningfulness of the response. Authors also placed multiple checks to ensure that the participants were attentive when labeling the results. CRB-CRS defeats all comparative models in this controlled evaluation experiment. However, results on only one dataset were evaluated and only 90 people participated in the evaluation. This limits the generality of their metrics. Additionally, such a 5-point scale is highly subjective which can lead to a huge variation on repeated evaluations.

There have been some attempts to make the evaluation framework public which can make the process cheaper albeit noisy. Recently, CRSArena \cite{CRSArena} was launched as a space on HuggingFace Hub which aims to assess the perception of a user regarding two conversational recommendation agents. The arena puts two anonymous systems from a pool of CRS in a pairwise battle. The aim of the user is to interact with both at the same time and then vote a winner. Although this aims to crowd-source the quality assessment of CRS in an interactive manner, perception of the user may be biased towards certain kinds of recommendation resulting in a skewed result. Also, the section of the people interacting with this tool may not be an indicative of reality and the performance seen may not translate to actual business performance.

\subsection{Other aspects}
Apart from the two components, CRS can be evaluated on aspects that improve the overall experience but are hard to quantify. To align CRS closely to user expectations, Michael in \cite{usercentriceval} highlights important paradigms a CRS must focus on. Even though performance metrics for recommendation and conversation are important, they do not directly correlate with a user-centric design. To drive user satisfaction, a CRS must be trusted by the user and should be able to incorporate user preferences and feedback in real-time. It should emphasize preference elicitation and utilize domain knowledge from external sources to assist users to arrive at a relevant recommendation.

\section{Fairness and Ethics}
With the rise of transformers and LLMs, it is evident that many CRS systems may incorporate them especially as part of their conversational module. A lot of effort is being made to utilize open source and other large language models for recommendations either by finetuning them or via agents \cite{llmimpact, MACRS}. Since a transformer architecture is inherently black box, a comprehensive study is required to assess their fairness and biases. A common approach is to utilize pretrained LLMs for recommendations using few-shot learning but that often leads to poor item fairness and coverage as noted by authors of \cite{chatgptbias}. As seen in \cite{Lee2014ImpactOR, DCC_RecSysImpact}, recommendation systems can conform user preferences. Hence, this study is necessary for recommendation systems but is missing from all models evaluated in this survey.

\section{Conclusion}
The field of CRS continues to evolve rapidly, driven by advances in artificial intelligence, natural language processing, and human-computer interaction. As these technologies mature, we can expect to see increasingly sophisticated systems that provide more personalized, natural, and effective recommendation experiences across diverse application domains. However, a significant portion of work is required to make these systems much more generalizable, diverse and fair.

Although real-world datasets and applications are still being experimented upon, the continued development of CRS will play a crucial role in shaping how users interact with information systems and discover relevant content in an increasingly complex digital landscape. With the increasing appearance of LLM based chat bots on major platforms, CRS can facilitate utilizing the same to drive users to better products.
